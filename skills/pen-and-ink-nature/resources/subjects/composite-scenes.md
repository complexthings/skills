# Composite scenes

Full landscapes, seasonal scenes, wooden posts and fences, layering and tonal casting.

## What makes a composite scene work

**Layering is the compositional method.** Place a small foreground element, then progressively
larger elements behind it — a foreground bush, then taller trees, then background pines, then a
mountain and sky. The eye travels those layers, and that travel is the appeal of the picture.

Two supporting rules:

- **Every scene wants a backdrop the eye can rest on.** Angled parallel lines are the simplest
  one, and usually enough.
- **Elements are cast for their tonal role, not just their species.** Pines are dark, so they are
  what you put behind light spring growth. A wooded backdrop is darkened so that flowing water in
  front of it reads bright. Choose the element that gives you the tone the composition needs.

## Structural approach

### Building the scene

1. Choose the layers front to back and decide the tonal role of each before drawing anything. Name
   which layer is the darkest and which is the brightest.
2. Draw the small foreground element first. It sets the scale for everything behind it.
3. Work backward, each layer larger in extent and carrying fewer marks than the one in front.
4. Add the backdrop last if the scene needs a resting field.
5. Check the white. Leave a margin wherever one layer overlaps another; without it the layers
   merge into a dark mass.

Depth is coded by **size and density**, never by blur. Farther elements are smaller, carry fewer
marks, and drop tonal steps — three tones become two, two become a solid tapered dark.

### Seasonal signatures

**Spring.** Light, airy foliage on visible twigs, denser toward the stem ends. Dots and ticks for
new growth. Wild flowers and light ground cover below. Set against dark pines for contrast.

**Winter.** Draw the foreground snow edge first as a flattish shape. Snow gets the snow-peak
treatment — reserved paper with irregular darkening along its edges. Elements sit partly hidden
behind that snow edge, which is what sells the depth of the snow. Bare trees or snow-laden pines
behind.

**Summer or full season.** Dense three-tone foliage masses, light at the top and dark at the
bottom.

### Wooden posts

Technically a trunk — same roundness logic, same tonal steps across the width — but weathered
rather than barked. Four distinct textures, each giving a different age:

1. **Small wandering lines.** The trunk stroke reused. Reads as a sound post.
2. **Crisscrossing angular lines.** Loose and non-uniform, never a ruled mesh. Reads as splitting
   grain and a more weathered post.
3. **Tapered crevices alone**, with the surrounding grain left implied. The sparest treatment and
   often the most convincing.
4. **Flowing grain lines running around a deep knot.** Use where a knot is the focal point.

A rectangular post darkens the face turned away from the light rather than grading across a curve.
Receding posts shrink with distance and give strong depth cheaply. A fence is posts set at an
angle joined by wire; more crevices read as more decay.

## Prompt fragment

```
A spring landscape in pen and ink, pure black ink on white paper, no grey wash and no colour,
composed as receding layers. In the foreground, a low bush and a patch of grass strokes on a
ground plane of horizontal parallel lines. Behind it, deciduous trees in light airy foliage
drawn as dots and ticks on visible twigs, denser toward the stem ends. Behind those, a band of
dark pines drawn in short mirrored needle strokes, dark enough to throw the spring growth
forward. A distant mountain behind the pines, fewer planes and less detail than anything in
front of it, toned with parallel lines. The sky is left white. Each layer is smaller in mark
count than the one in front, and a thin white margin separates each layer from the one behind
it. Hand-drawn irregularity throughout, no ruled lines, sparse coverage with most of the paper
left as bare white.
```

For a winter scene, replace the foreground clause: *a flattish snow edge across the foreground
drawn as reserved white paper with irregular darkening along its upper edge, with the bases of the
elements behind partly hidden by it, and bare trees and snow-laden pines beyond.*

## Failure modes

| Symptom | Correction |
|---|---|
| Scene reads flat | Name the layers front to back and reduce mark count with distance |
| Layers merge into a dark mass | Leave a white margin at every overlap |
| Distant elements too detailed | Drop tonal steps with distance: three tones, then two, then solid |
| No focal contrast | Cast an element for its tone: dark pines behind light growth, dark backdrop behind bright water |
| Detail level too fine for the format | State the small drawing size; coarse marks and sparse coverage |
| Fence posts identical | Shrink receding posts and vary the crevice count |
