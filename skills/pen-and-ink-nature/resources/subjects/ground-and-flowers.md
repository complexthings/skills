# Ground and flowers

Grass, ground plane, paths, wild flowers, flowers close up.

## What makes ground read as ground

**Grass alone floats.** A field of grass strokes on bare paper has no surface under it. The ground
plane — horizontal parallel lines whose length increases toward the viewer — is what the grass
stands on, and it has to be there before the grass reads as growing.

Two rules govern everything at this scale:

- **Suggest, never cover.** Grass covers part of the ground, not all of it. Density varies in
  organic patches, never in circles or straight bands, and any emergent pattern kills the effect.
- **Scale against neighbours.** Grass is much smaller than a stone. Getting the relative size wrong
  is more damaging than getting the stroke wrong.

Flowers have their own controlling idea: **softness comes from broken line.** A hard continuous
outline destroys the delicacy that makes a flower read as a flower, which is why close-up flower
work uses dotted and dashed contours throughout.

## Structural approach

### Ground plane

1. Lay horizontal parallel lines for the ground. Make them **longer toward the viewer** and
   shorter as they recede; that length gradient is the depth cue.
2. Curve those lines instead of ruling them straight wherever the ground should undulate. The
   curvature declares the slope.
3. Draw the grass **on** that plane, not floating above it.

A path is made by running parallel lines in from both edges and leaving white down the middle. The
white leads the eye. Darken the path's edges slightly so the white reads as a surface rather than
a gap.

### Grass

The stroke is short, slightly curved, and rises from a root. Three rules:

- Shrink strokes with distance.
- Scale them against the other objects in the scene.
- Distribute them irregularly. Vary density in organic patches; break up any pattern that starts
  to emerge.

Darken the grass at the base of a trunk. That small dark is what grounds the tree — see
[trees.md](trees.md).

### Wild flowers

1. Draw a slightly curved stem. Never straight; always a small wiggle.
2. Add a simple bloom. At this scale the bloom is a shape, not a study.
3. Put a wiggly line at the base to ground the stem, plus a hint of grass around it.
4. Leave white around the flower wherever it overlaps anything else, or the drawing turns to a
   dark mess.

For flowers against a dark background, draw the **background around them** and leave the flowers
as bare paper. At distance, drop individual flowers entirely and give a general suggestion of
flowers instead.

### Flowers close up

A different discipline from wild flowers, and the only place in this style where a subject gets
sustained detail.

1. Draw the outline as a **broken, dotted or dashed line**. This is not optional; a hard
   continuous outline is what makes a close flower read as a cut-out.
2. Texture the petals with dots and ticks.
3. Give each petal form with **soft** contour lines — also broken.
4. Tone to an assumed light direction: fewer marks where the petal faces the light, denser dots
   and ticks where it curves away.

## Prompt fragment

```
A patch of wild flowers on grassy ground in pen and ink, pure black ink on white paper, no
grey wash and no colour. The ground plane is horizontal parallel lines, longer and more widely
spaced toward the viewer, curving gently to show the ground undulating. Grass is short,
slightly curved strokes rising from roots on that plane, covering only part of the ground,
grouped in irregular organic patches with no repeating pattern, and shrinking with distance.
Flower stems are slightly curved with a small wiggle, never straight, each with a wiggly line
and a hint of grass at its base. A thin white margin is left around every flower where it
overlaps the grass behind. Hand-drawn irregularity throughout, no ruled lines, most of the
paper left as bare white.
```

For a close flower study, replace the body: *a single flower head filling the frame, outlined in a
broken dotted line rather than a continuous one, petals textured with fine dots and ticks and
given form by soft broken contour lines, with fewer marks on the petal faces turned toward the
light and denser dots where each petal curves away.*

## Failure modes

| Symptom | Correction |
|---|---|
| Grass floats | Add a ground plane of horizontal parallel lines under it |
| Ground reads flat | Vary line length toward the viewer; curve the lines for undulation |
| Grass looks like a lawn texture | Cover only part of the ground; vary density in organic patches |
| A pattern emerges in the grass | Break it deliberately; irregular distribution is the whole effect |
| Grass the same size everywhere | Shrink strokes with distance and scale against nearby objects |
| Close flower reads as a cut-out | Break the outline into dots and dashes |
| Flowers merge with the background | Leave a white margin at every overlap |
