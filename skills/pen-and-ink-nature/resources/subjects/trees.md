# Trees

Trunks, bark, branches, deciduous foliage, pine foliage.

## What makes a tree read as a tree

One contrast carries the whole subject: **bark marks run along the trunk and are line-like;
foliage marks are closed or looping and have no dominant direction.** Blur that distinction and
the drawing reads as a shrub with a stick in it.

Three further tests a finished tree has to pass:

- The trunk is round, not flat. Roundness comes from tonal steps across the width, never from an
  outline.
- The branch structure is decided before any texture goes down. Texture on an unresolved armature
  reads as noise.
- The tree is grounded. A few grass strokes at the base stop it floating on the paper.

## Structural approach

### Trunk

1. Draw the trunk's two edges. Neither is straight and neither is parallel to the other.
2. Set the tonal steps across the width before any texture. A near trunk takes **three tones** —
   roughly a third darkest, a third middle, a third left near-bare. A sapling or a distant trunk
   takes **two tones**: darken the left and right edges, the lower one more, and leave a white
   streak down the centre. That streak is the roundness.
3. Lay bark on top of the tone, not instead of it. The bark mark is a wandering line running the
   trunk's length. A straight line will not do; the wander is the effect.
4. Add tapered crevices and ragged marks along the silhouette edges. More of them, heavier, reads
   as an older tree.
5. Ground it with grass strokes at the base and a slight darkening where trunk meets ground.

Bark escalates with proximity, in four steps: suggested grooves only; outlined bark plates each
toned and darkened at one end; raised ridges drawn as the subject themselves; explicit deep
crevices for the roughest species. Characterise a species by changing the mark, not by adding
more of it — birch is a different stroke, not a busier trunk.

### Branches

1. Decide primary and secondary branches first. Draw them as bare lines.
2. Texture each branch along its own direction, not the trunk's.
3. At every crossing, break the line of the branch behind. A one-millimetre gap at the
   intersection buys the depth that shading cannot.
4. Darken the joint where a side branch leaves a main one — slightly. Filled-solid joints are the
   commonest branch error.
5. Thin branches take the two-tone recipe with the reserved centre streak. End twigs drop to one
   tone: a solid tapered dark.

A silhouetted far tree is a two-tone main trunk plus solid tapered lines for everything else.

### Deciduous foliage

Foliage is organised into **masses**, never into leaves. The base mark is an open loop — a leaf
seen from far enough away that only its outline survives — drawn at varied sizes and orientations.

Two construction orders work:

- **Masses first:** lay the foreground masses, then add darker marks in the gaps behind them.
- **Fill then carve:** fill the crown outline with a light even tone, then darken into it to cut
  masses out. This is the only one that works at small size.

Light comes from overhead, so the crown is lighter at the top and darker at the bottom. Two rules
admit no exception: no straightness anywhere, in a mass or in the crown outline; and no regular
closed shape for a mass — break every mass edge with small swirls and ticks.

Species variation comes from swapping the mark: small angular lines, loopy lines, loose scribble,
or, for a middle-distance tree, plain parallel lines inside a foliage-shaped outline with one side
darkened.

### Pine foliage

The needle stroke is short, curved and angled outward and downward, **mirrored about the tree's
centre** — right-leaning on the right side, left-leaning on the left. Two rules govern the mass:
it is darker at its centre and thins toward its edges, and within one branch the underside is
darker than the top.

Never draw a line across the needle direction to close the mass. The edge of a pine mass is made
of needle ends and nothing else.

Two approaches: **dense**, where needles hide the branch armature entirely, and **branch-based**,
where the armature is drawn first and needle clusters hang off it. Alternating lighter and darker
bands of needles reads as separate tiers. A snow-laden pine is the same strokes with white left
where snow sits.

## Prompt fragment

```
A single deciduous tree in pen and ink, pure black ink on white paper, no grey wash and no
colour. The near trunk is rendered in three tones across its width — one third darkest, one
third middle, one third left as bare paper — with wandering bark lines running along its
length and tapered crevices cut into the body. Branch structure is resolved before texture:
primary and secondary branches, the line of the rear branch broken at every crossing. Thin
branches are two tone, darkened along both edges with a white streak reserved down the
centre. The crown is built of scribble strokes in open leaf loops, grouped into irregular
masses, lighter at the top and darker at the bottom, with white gaps left between masses to
read as background foliage. Every mass edge is broken with small swirls and tick marks; no
straight line and no regular closed shape anywhere in the foliage. Grass strokes at the base
ground the trunk. Hand-drawn irregularity throughout, no ruled or mechanical lines.
```

Swap the crown for pines by replacing the scribble clause: *short curved needle strokes angled
outward and downward, mirrored left and right of the tree's centre, the mass darker at its centre
and thinning to needle ends at its edges, with no line drawn across the needle direction.*

## Failure modes

| Symptom | Correction |
|---|---|
| Trunk reads flat | Name the tonal count and where the lightest band sits, not "shading" |
| Foliage reads as a bush | Foliage marks must be looping and closed; bark marks must run along the trunk |
| Crown has a clean silhouette | Break the boundary with swirls and ticks along the edge |
| Branch joints are black blobs | Darken the joint slightly, never fill it |
| Twigs read as flat wires | Restate the reserved white centre streak |
| Tree floats | Add grass strokes at the base |
