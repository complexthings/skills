# Water

Still water, moving water, flow toward the viewer, waterfalls, rivers and streams, shoreline and
waves.

## What makes water read as water

**The sharpness of a reflection's edges tells the viewer how still the water is.** Crisp,
clean-edged reflection reads as calm. The more turbulent the surface, the hazier and more
distorted the reflection becomes. Every other decision in a water drawing follows from that one.

Two supporting tests:

- The surface is **horizontal**. Water is drawn with horizontal marks, and the reflection is the
  only place vertical marks appear.
- Water is mostly untouched paper. A surface marked edge to edge stops reading as a liquid and
  starts reading as a textured floor.

## Structural approach

### Still water

1. Lay sparse horizontal wavy lines across the surface, with a few scattered dots. Leave most of
   the paper bare and let no pattern emerge.
2. Draw the reflection with **vertical** lines, clean-edged, densest at the base of the reflected
   object and thinning upward.

Three reflection rules the drawing has to obey:

- An object set back from the water's edge is only **partly** reflected.
- An object leaning at an angle reflects at a matching angle. Approximate is fine — the direction
  matters, the degree does not.
- Reflection fades with height. The twig ends of a reflected tree are barely present; indicate
  them with dots and ticks, not with lines.

### Moving water

Same horizontal surface stroke with more wave in it. The reflection changes from vertical clean
lines to **horizontal distorted** ones. Darker plus more distorted equals more turbulent.

An alternative that stays inside one stroke family: build the reflection from the water stroke
itself, packed denser. Because that stroke is horizontal, this is inherently a turbulent-water
treatment and cannot be used for calm.

### Water flowing toward the viewer

Not the water stroke. Use the **ripple stroke**: curves that grow larger as they travel away from
their source while growing weaker. Put darks near the source and lighten with distance.

### Falling water

1. Draw the fall as slightly curved, broken lines with a small bulge. A flat vertical fall reads
   as a wall.
2. Add a tapered dark inside the fall for volume.
3. Break the straightness of the fall's edges with dots and ticks.
4. Darken the background behind the fall. The fall stays bright because everything around it got
   darker, not because it was drawn lighter.

### River or stream

A **tapered** outline is more interesting than a parallel one — let the channel narrow as it
recedes. One bank is usually visible: draw its depth with vertical parallel lines, longer lines
for a deeper bank. Indicate the water itself with a few flowing lines that follow the bend.

### Shoreline and waves

1. Lay the shore's curve first. The wave's curve follows it, so the order is not optional.
2. A larger wave has a continuous front line. Smaller waves break, so their front is irregular and
   broken.
3. Darken the wave front irregularly with dots and ticks.
4. For a second wave behind the first, leave white between them.

For a crashing wave, the **angle of the broken lines encodes motion**: near-horizontal reads as a
gentle arrival, tilted upward reads as impact. Mist and bounce-back are dots and ticks.

## Prompt fragment

```
A still pond in pen and ink, pure black ink on white paper, no grey wash and no colour. The
water surface is sparse horizontal wavy lines with a few scattered dots, most of the paper
left untouched, no repeating pattern. A tree on the far bank is reflected in clean-edged
vertical parallel lines, densest at the base of the reflection and fading upward, with the
reflected twig ends indicated only by dots and ticks rather than lines. The reflection sits
directly below the tree and matches its lean. The visible bank's depth is drawn with short
vertical parallel lines. Hand-drawn irregularity in every stroke, no ruled lines and no
mechanical repetition.
```

Turn the same scene turbulent by replacing the reflection clause: *the reflection is drawn in
horizontal distorted lines rather than clean verticals, darker and more broken, with the surface
strokes carrying more wave in them.*

## Failure modes

| Symptom | Correction |
|---|---|
| Water reads as a textured floor | Cut the surface marks back; most of the water is bare paper |
| Calm water with a broken reflection | Match reflection edge sharpness to surface state — crisp for calm |
| Reflection as dark as the object | Reflection fades with height; fade the top to dots |
| Waterfall drawn as grey texture | Reserve the fall as white and darken the background behind it |
| Wave front too clean | Break it with dots and ticks; only large waves keep a continuous front line |
| Wave drawn before the shore | Lay the shore curve first; the wave follows it |
